Diagram language

How Kerf draws: what each line weight and pattern means, how dimensions and angles are labeled, how a drawing points at a row of the cut list, and how clearance zones, reference faces, and long parts are shown, on a screen and on paper.

Three directions, each drawing the four views PRODUCT.md asks for (isometric, exploded, per-part cut details, site layout) for the worked example. Every drawing on this page is generated from the template's geometry by a scratch script, and every number in it is a string from core's snapshot or core's formatters. The drawings use #42's typeface and #43's C1 colors, and they are drawn for the width they are shown at: on a phone you see the phone version.

What I need from you

Three choices. My suggestion is first in each. A reply like D2, letters, action steps is enough.

  1. Diagram language for the plan: D2 shop plan, D1 drafting, or D3 action.
  2. How drawings name parts: letters matching the cut list (A to E), item numbers in balloons, or names written out.
  3. Drawings in Building mode: action steps (D3's style inside the chosen language: one significant part per step, the rest in light line), or the plan's exploded view with the step's parts highlighted.

Best judged on your phone, and once printed. The controls above the directions switch light and dark, show the phone versions on a wide screen, and simulate grayscale print and glare.

Common to all three

What every direction does

Numbers from core only

Every length and angle in a drawing is a string core formatted (hard rule 1, #42's rules for numbers): 131-1/2", 20.0°, 18' 8-7/8". A drawing never measures itself.

Drawn for the width shown

Lines and text are sized in screen pixels, not scaled with the drawing (SVG vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke). A narrow container gets its own layout: labels in one column, a key under the drawing, vertical dimensions written along the line. Text never drops below 13 px.

Angles as the saw is set

Every end cut shows the setting from square, boxed and larger, with the angle to the edge smaller and labeled (#42), and an arc from a square line in the drawing itself, so it is clear which face the angle is measured from (DOMAIN.md, Units).

Never color alone

#43, rule 6. The selected part is tinted and outlined more heavily; zones have a dashed edge and a label, and hatching in print; below-grade wood is dashed.

Long parts break

A 131-1/2" leg is 3-1/2" wide. Drawn whole on a phone it is about 9 px tall, so D1 and D2 break the middle out (the ASME long-break line) and draw both ends at a readable scale. The overall dimension still spans the break.

Text equivalent

Each drawing has a label generated from the same data ("Cut detail for the back leg: overall 131-1/2", both ends cut level at 20.0°..."), and the cut list is the full text equivalent, as W3C's guidance for complex images recommends.

D1 · Drafting

Engineering-drawing conventions: one ink, line weight and pattern carry meaning

From the subject
The conventions of engineering drawings. Lines follow ASME Y14.2: thick visible outlines, thin dimension, extension, and leader lines, dashed hidden lines (the legs below grade), chain center lines (the bolt axis), and phantom lines for parts that are not in the cut list (chains and seats). Dimensions follow ASME Y14.5: arrowheads about three times as long as they are wide, gaps where extension lines leave the part, and text that reads from the bottom of the sheet. Parts carry numbered balloons, as on an assembly drawing with a parts list. The reference face carries a datum symbol, and long parts are drawn with conventional break lines.
Principles
Never color alone (#43, rule 6): every meaning is in line weight and pattern, so the drawings print on any printer and read the same with any color vision. The smallest effective difference (Tufte): two weights and four patterns are enough to separate every role.
In use
Laptop and print: the clearest of the three on paper. Garage: thin lines at 0.9 px fade under glare, and the white faces give no sense of which side of a leg you are looking at, so the isometric reads as a tangle of outlines at phone size. A woodworker does not read a datum triangle, and balloon numbers mean nothing until you find the list.
Cost
Looks like CAD output, which #39 turned down for the laptop (L-C). Item numbers carry no meaning of their own; the parts list is the only key.

D2 · Shop plan

Woodworking-magazine plans: shaded faces, lettered parts, dimensions in their own color

From the subject
Woodworking magazine plans, such as Woodsmith's: shaded faces, parts lettered with the same letters as the materials list, dimensioned part details, and an exploded view with guidelines to where each part goes. Underneath, the line conventions are D1's (hidden, center, phantom, break lines), because they mean the same thing to anyone who has read a plan.
Principles
Shading tells the eye which way a surface faces, and it survives grayscale where hue does not. Three tones step about 12 in lightness (L* 100, 88, 76 in light), enough to separate faces at phone size without competing with the ink. Dimensions take the dimension color from #43, so numbers stand apart from geometry; they also keep their arrows and extension lines, so color is never the only signal. Letters are short enough for a phone and match the cut list, which on screen links both ways (#39, Q3 a).
In use
Laptop: the most readable at a glance. Store: letters match the cut list, so "C" on a board card and "C" in the drawing are the same part. Garage: the solid faces hold up at arm's length; the selected part is tinted and outlined more heavily. Print: tones print as grays, and the zone tint becomes hatching.
Measured
Ink on the darkest face tone 8.8:1 light, 7.0:1 dark. Dimension color on the page 6.6:1 and 7.6:1. Under 20% glare the face tones still differ (1.7:1 light), but in dark mode they flatten (1.2:1), so the outlines carry the shape.
Cost
Letters need core to assign them, stably, in the cut list. The tones need their own values in dark mode, where mixing toward the ink lightens instead of darkening. More ink on the page than D1 or D3.

D3 · Action

Assembly-instruction style: the part being worked on, strong, in light context

From the subject
Assembly instructions, as IKEA and LEGO draw them, and the design principles Agrawala and colleagues tested with people assembling furniture: one significant part per step, every new part visible, and action diagrams that pull the new part away and show where it goes. Everything already built is drawn in light line, the part being worked on strong and filled. Parts are named, not keyed. The site layout shows where to drive stakes.
Principles
Action diagrams were easier to follow than diagrams of the finished assembly in Agrawala's tests, with less time and fewer errors. Names need no lookup. Big numbers follow #42's shop sizes.
In use
Garage: the best of the three for a build step, because it shows only what is happening now. Laptop and print: weak as a plan's reference drawings. The assembled view has no dimensions, the leg drawn whole at true proportion is a sliver (131-1/2" by 3-1/2"), and the site layout without a zone fill is easy to misread. The step order shown is only to illustrate the style; real steps come from M6 (#52).
Measured
The light context line (the hidden-line gray) is 4.8:1 on the page in light and 4.6:1 in dark, but under 20% glare it falls to 3.0:1 and 1.9:1, the same reason #43 removed secondary text from the shop modes.
Cost
Needs build steps with their parts (#52) before it can draw anything but the assembled view. Names crowd a phone-width drawing. The stake layout needs numbers core does not compute yet (diagonals to check the footprint is square).

Suggested: D2 for the plan, with action steps in Building mode

D2 reads fastest at every size and prints in gray without losing anything, and its letters tie the drawings to the cut list in a way a woodworker already knows. It keeps D1's line conventions, since those mean the same thing on every plan. Building mode borrows D3's idea, not its thin context lines: the step's part is drawn strong and filled, the rest in D2's normal ink at a lighter tone, so the context survives glare.

Linking drawings to the cut list

How drawings name parts

#39 chose part ids on every drawing primitive (Q3 a), so tapping a part in a drawing selects its cut-list row and the reverse. Paper needs a printed key as well.

Letters matching the cut listSuggested

A to E in cut-list order, shown in the cut list's first column and in every drawing. The woodworking-magazine convention. Short enough for a phone; one letter per part, shared by both instances of a pair. Needs core to assign them, stably within a template version.

Item numbers in balloons

The engineering-drawing convention (a find number tied to a parts list). The same length as letters, but numbers sit beside lengths and quantities everywhere in Kerf, so "2" could be a count.

Names written out

No lookup. "Back leg" takes about 70 px, so a phone drawing gives a third of its width to labels.

Building mode

Drawings in a build step

#40 gave every step one layout: where you are, what to do, the numbers, the drawing, the method, the flags. The question is what that drawing shows.

Action stepsSuggested

Only what this step adds, pulled away from where it goes, with a motion arrow; everything already built in a lighter tone. Agrawala and colleagues found people assembled faster and with fewer errors from diagrams like these than from diagrams of the finished assembly. Needs each step's parts (#52).

The exploded view, highlighted

One drawing for every step, with the step's parts tinted. Nothing new to build, but every step shows every part, and the reader has to work out what changed.

The suggestion, drawn: action steps in D2's language. The context keeps D2's face tones and ink, so it holds up under glare; the step's part is tinted and outlined heavily. The order is only an illustration.

If the suggestions are taken

What M5 builds to. Screen widths are CSS pixels and do not scale with the drawing; print widths follow ASME Y14.2's two weights, thick about twice thin. Colors are C1 roles (#43).

Faces

Three tones by which way a face points: top lightest, front and back the middle tone, ends darkest, in both themes. The selected part uses the highlight color in the same three steps.

Text

Atkinson Hyperlegible Next. Dimensions 14 px semibold in the dimension color, with a halo of the page color so lines never cross them; labels 14 px; notes 13 px. In Building mode, the lengths you cut to are 20 px bold. Text reads from the bottom of the sheet; on narrow drawings vertical dimensions are written along the line instead (ASME Y14.5's aligned system). Print: 9 pt, never under 8 pt.

Part letters

A dark rounded square with the letter in the page color, tied to the part by a leader. Letters sit in columns beside the drawing, sorted by height so leaders do not cross; on a phone, one column.

Reference face

The woodworker's face mark, a loop, on the face the angle is measured from. Which face that is becomes part data in #50.

Cut details

The part's face in its own plane, its long axis across the page. Overall length above, edge lengths below, marks along the edge they are measured on. Waste from a taper cut drawn dashed. Both ends shown at a readable scale with a break when the part is more than about 10 times longer than wide.

Site layout

Plan view with the front, downhill, at the bottom. Feet and inches for distances over 6 feet. The area to keep clear tinted on screen and hatched on paper, its source printed under the drawing.

Precedents and guidance

Sources

Checked in October 2026. ASME standards are sold, not published free; their line types and widths here come from the published line-type tables and teaching summaries linked below, and the exact print widths should be checked against the standard before M5 fixes them.

Line conventions

ASME Y14.2 line types (visible, hidden, center, phantom, dimension, extension, leader, break) and two widths, thick about twice thin (0.6 and 0.3 mm in common summaries).

FreeCAD TechDraw: ASME Y14.2-2008 line types · FAA AMT study guide: drawing lines

Dimensions and parts lists

ASME Y14.5 dimensioning (unidirectional and aligned systems); ASME Y14.34 parts lists, with find numbers that balloons point to.

ASME Y14.34 · Dimensioning procedures syllabus

Woodworking plans

Woodsmith's cutting diagrams use "the same letter designations found in the materials list", with quantities, so every drawn part traces to a list row.

Woodsmith: creating cutting diagrams

Assembly instructions

Agrawala, Phan, Heiser, Haymaker, Klingner, Hanrahan, and Tversky (SIGGRAPH 2003): step-by-step diagrams, one significant part at a time, new parts always visible, action diagrams over structural ones.

Designing Effective Step-By-Step Assembly Instructions

Reference faces

The face side is marked with a loop and the face edge with a V joined to it; measurements are taken from those faces.

Marking out wood · Common Woodworking: registration faces

Use zones

Equipment at least 6 feet from structures; in front of and behind swings, twice the height of the top bar (CPSC Pub. 324, p. 2).

CPSC Outdoor Home Playground Safety Handbook

Emphasis

Make visual distinctions as small as they can be while still clear (Tufte's smallest effective difference); shading conveys which way a surface faces.

Boxes and Arrows: three lessons from Tufte · Gooch et al., SIGGRAPH 1998

SVG and accessibility

Non-scaling strokes keep line weights fixed at any size. Complex images get a short label and a long description in the page.
