Kerf · Design milestone · Issue #40

Information architecture

What lives where, and how you move between planning on the laptop, shopping on the phone, and building in the garage. This builds on the choices from #39: the project page reads like the printed plan, editing is a split screen with live output, the store view is a checklist by aisle with board cards, and the garage view is one step at a time with cutting done board by board.

Three directions for the overall structure follow, each with a site map, how navigation works on each device and on paper, and phone and laptop sketches. Two smaller choices sit below them: the order of a project's sections, and what a build step shows. Numbers in the sketches come from the worked example, as in #39.

Laptop frames are wider than a phone. On a phone, scroll them sideways inside their box.

Decided, October 2026

The owner took the suggestion for all three: IA-2, order A, steps as shown. The key screens redrawn on that structure are under Revised key screens; reasons and rejected directions are in docs/design/README.md. The choices were:

  1. Structure: IA-2 one plan with Shop and Build launched from it, IA-1 Plan, Shop, Build as tabs inside each project, or IA-3 Plan, Shop, Build as the app's top level.
  2. Order of a project's sections: A decide first (structural review near the top), or B build order (structural review as an appendix).
  3. What a build step shows: the cutting and assembly step layouts as drawn, or changes.

Common to every direction

What stays the same whichever structure you pick

Two global places

Projects and Tools. Tools (the inventory) is shared by every project, since methods are chosen from it. Log out sits with them. Login is outside the app.

Planning never changes progress; shop views never change the plan

Reading and editing the plan touch parameters only. Ticking a shopping line or finishing a step touches progress only (#57). Each screen does one or the other, so a mis-tap in the garage cannot edit the design.

Status follows what you do

A project is Planned until you start shopping or building, then In progress, then Built when you mark the last step done (PRODUCT.md, feature 8). You can still set it by hand.

Regenerating warns once progress exists

Fitbod locks a workout once you tap Start Workout, so it is not regenerated under you. Kerf already regenerates only on request (0008); once you have ticked anything, the request says how much progress would reset before you confirm.

One project response

Every view of a project renders from the same response, so a brief drop leaves all of them readable (Q5 in #39).

Print is the plan

Printing any view of a project prints the plan document in its section order, each diagram and the cut list on its own page (M5). Shop and Build views print as the plan too; their state is for the screen.

Platform convention (Apple HIG tab bars; NN/g mobile navigation)
A tab bar is the standard way to move between a few peer sections, and three fits well inside the usual limit of five. It stays visible, so Shop and Build are always one tap away.
Two layers of navigation
The tab bar belongs to a project, and Projects and Tools sit above it in the header. That is two systems on one small screen, and the tabs vanish when you leave the project.
Peers that are not peers
On the laptop, Shop and Build are rarely used but always shown. On the phone in the garage, Plan is rarely used but always shown. The tabs treat three unequal tasks as equal.
Mode signal
Build looks like the other tabs apart from the underline, so it is easy to forget that taps there change progress. NN/g advises at least two signals for a mode.

Precedent: a recipe page and its cook mode; Fitbod's Start Workout
Recipe sites keep one page for the recipe and offer a cook mode for following it at the stove. Fitbod plans a workout, then you start it and log sets in a focused view. Planning and doing are different tasks with different screens, and the doing screen is entered on purpose.
Modes done the way NN/g advises
A mode is clearly named, entered deliberately, and signalled two ways: the dark bar with its name, and the absence of other navigation. It is also safe by construction, since a mode can change progress but never the plan.
Hub and spoke for task sessions (NN/g)
A trip to the store or an evening in the garage is one task per session, which is where NN/g says a hub works. The hub is the plan, and the Resume card on the projects list skips it when you are mid-build.
Cost
Moving from Shopping to Building means going back through the plan; that is rare. Reopening into the same mode needs the URL to carry the mode and step, which hash routes already allow.

Fastest in the garage
Opening the app on the phone and tapping Build lands on the step, with no project to pick. Paprika and Fitbod put their activities (grocery list, workout) at the top level the same way.
A hidden piece of state
"Current project" decides what Shop and Build show, and it is set somewhere else. With two projects in flight, the store list may be for the wrong one. That is the mode slip NN/g warns about, and it needs new stored state (a setting on the server).
The plan loses its place
Projects and the plan share one tab, so the laptop's main task sits one level down. The structure favors the phone over the laptop, where most planning happens.
Where it leads
It suits one shopping list across several projects, as Paprika merges recipes into one list. That is out of scope for the MVP.

Suggestion

IA-2. It keeps one structure, the plan, that matches the printed page and the laptop's main job, and it gives the store and the garage focused screens you enter on purpose. The Resume card covers IA-3's speed in the garage without a hidden current project. IA-1 is the conventional fallback if full-screen modes feel heavy once built.

Order of a project's sections

One order for the screen and the printed plan

The plan document (L-B in #39) needs a fixed section order, which is also the print order and the contents list. Woodsmith's published plans put the drawings first, then the materials list and cutting diagram, then the steps. The open question is where the structural review goes.

A · Decide firstSuggested
  1. Summary: key results, flags, version notice
  2. Diagrams: isometric, exploded, site layout
  3. Structural review: inputs needed, margins, checklist, flags
  4. Cut list, by part, with cut details
  5. Boards: which pieces come from which board
  6. Shopping list
  7. Build steps
  8. Parameters and inputs

The review decides whether the design is ready to buy for, and it is what an experienced builder reads first on paper. Flags stay prominent (PRODUCT.md, principle 3). Buying and building follow in the order you do them.

B · Build order
  1. Summary
  2. Diagrams
  3. Shopping list
  4. Cut list and boards
  5. Build steps
  6. Structural review
  7. Parameters and inputs

Follows the project's life from store to garage, as a published plan does. The review becomes an appendix, so margins and the checklist are easier to skip, and the summary has to carry the flags alone.

Same skeleton for every step (Nielsen's fourth heuristic, consistency)
The eye learns where the numbers, the drawing, and the flag sit, and finds them at arm's length without reading the whole screen.
Small steps, each one action
Assembly instructions work by keeping each step to one action with its parts and hardware listed. A cutting step is one board; an assembly step is one joint or one subassembly.
All steps
The step list groups steps into phases (cutting, drilling, assembly, finishing) so you can see where you are and jump. That needs a phase on each step (new requirement below).

Precedents and guidance

Sources behind these directions

Checked in October 2026. Where a primary page could not be loaded, the note says so.

NN/g: Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation

Tab bars suit a few main sections, about five at most, and stay visible. A hub suits task-based apps where people do one task per session, at the cost of a step back to the hub. Basis for IA-1 and IA-2.

Budiu, 2015

NN/g: Modes in User Interfaces

Modes help when one input must mean several things, and hurt when a slip causes harm. Signal the mode at least two ways, name it clearly, and prefer separate controls where a mix-up matters. Basis for IA-2's mode bar and for keeping plan and progress on separate screens.

Laubheimer, 2019

Apple HIG: tab bars and sidebars

Tab bars are for navigation, not actions, and stay visible except inside a modal task; on iPad, consider a sidebar. I could not load Apple's page directly; this is from summaries that quote it.
