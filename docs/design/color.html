Kerf · Design milestone · Issue #43

Color

A palette with named roles, in light and dark, for text, surfaces, controls, flags, margins, input-needed states, and diagram ink. Every pairing is measured. Each direction is shown on the plan, a shop step, and a diagram, in Atkinson Hyperlegible (#42).

Kerf has a rule that shapes all of this: results are margins, never verdicts (hard rule 2). Red and green read as fail and pass, and red is the conventional color of danger, so neither appears near a structural result.

Decided, October 2026

The owner took the suggestion for all three: C1, follow the device, rules as written. Reasons and rejected directions are in docs/design/README.md. The choices were:

  1. Palette: C1 graphite and chalk line, C2 layout blue, or C3 high-visibility.
  2. Light and dark: follow the device, always dark in the shop modes, or a switch in the app (which needs a stored setting).
  3. Rules for color: approve the six rules, or change them.

The controls above the directions still simulate grayscale print, the two common forms of red-green color blindness, and glare.

Rules

What color may and may not say

1 · Margins have no color

A margin is shown in the text color, always with "under these assumptions". Coloring 1.1x differently from 3.5x would draw a line between acceptable and not, which is a verdict Kerf does not make.

2 · No red or green near structural results

Red is the color of danger in safety signage (ANSI Z535), and red and green read as fail and pass. Errors are crimson and appear only beside form fields; progress uses the accent, not green.

3 · Flags are amber, with an icon and a label

A flag means "ask an experienced builder", so it gets an attention color in the caution family, never danger red. It always carries a flag icon, a border, and the words "Expert check", so it reads in grayscale and to color-blind eyes.

4 · Input needed is not an error

A missing structural input is a question, not a mistake: a dashed accent outline and the words "Input needed". Errors are reserved for values that cannot be used.

5 · No secondary text in the shop modes

Measured under 20% glare, gray secondary text falls to 2.6 to 3.5:1 in C1 and C2. In Shopping and Building every word uses the main text color; hierarchy comes from size and weight.

6 · Never color alone

Every state has a second signal: flags an icon and label, errors an icon and message, input needed a dashed outline and label, done a check mark, the selected part a heavier outline as well as a fill, and clearance zones a dashed edge and label (hatching in print, #44).

Palette

Three directions, light and dark

Built in OKLCH, so equal steps in lightness look equal, then mapped into sRGB so screens and printers show the same colors. All three meet WCAG 2.2 AA for every pairing listed, in both themes.

From the subject
A warm, faintly wood-toned paper, text the color of graphite, and one blue taken from a chalk line: the marks a woodworker already makes on a board. Amber flags and sage clearance zones are the only other hues.
Measured
Text on page 16.0:1 light and 15.5:1 dark; the lowest text pairing 5.6:1. Under 20% glare, text holds 4.6:1 (light) and 4.2:1 (dark); secondary text drops to 3.5 and 2.6, which is why rule 5 keeps it out of the shop modes.
Grayscale and color blindness
The accent sits clearly apart from text in lightness, so links survive a black-and-white printer. The flag's fill is close to the card in gray, so in print the flag relies on its border, icon, and label (rule 6). Cost
Restrained. The character comes from type and layout more than color.

Suggestion

C1. It keeps the screen calm (warm paper, graphite text, one blue), so the few things that need attention stand out, and both themes hold up under glare and in grayscale. C2's dark theme is the most evocative, a blueprint, but it is the weakest in grayscale and its accent is too close to its text. C3 resists glare best; take its lesson as rule 5 rather than its colors.

Light and dark

Which theme, when

NN/g's review of the research: for people with normal vision, dark text on a light background reads better, and the advantage grows as text gets smaller and at night; in bright daylight the difference disappears. Its advice is to default to light, always offer dark, and use the operating system's setting.

Follow the deviceSuggested

Light or dark comes from the phone's or laptop's own setting, including its sunset schedule. Nothing to store. Print is always light.

Always dark in the shop modes

Less glare from a bright screen in a dim garage. Works against the research for small text, and the garage is not always dim.

A switch in the app

Light, dark, or follow the device, set in Kerf. A setting kept for every device needs storing on the server (0005), which is a new requirement.

Precedents and guidance

Sources

Checked in October 2026. Contrast measured with Color.js; color blindness simulated with the Machado, Oliveira, and Fernandes (2009) matrices at full severity.

WCAG 2.2 contrast

4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text (24 px, or 18.5 px bold) and for the edges of controls and graphics. Still the enforceable standard. APCA was removed from the WCAG 3 draft in 2023 and the WCAG 3 algorithm is undecided, so Lc values here are for reference.

WCAG 1.4.3 · WCAG 1.4.11 · Roselli: WCAG 3 contrast, April 2026

Light and dark

Light mode performs better for normal vision, more so for small text and at night; offer dark mode through the system setting.

NN/g: Dark Mode vs. Light Mode

Dim light

As light fades, reds darken first and blue-greens hold up (the Purkinje effect). A red signal is the first to disappear in a dark garage, especially on paper.

Purkinje effect · Britannica

Safety colors

ANSI Z535: red for danger, orange for warning, yellow for caution, blue for notice. Kerf's flags borrow the caution family and never the danger red.

ANSI Z535.1 overview

Color vision

Red-green color blindness affects up to about 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry; rates vary by population.

National Eye Institute

Tools

Color.js for conversion, gamut mapping, and contrast; OKLCH, widely supported in CSS.

Color.js · MDN: oklch()
