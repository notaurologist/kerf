Layout and grid

The spatial system: grid, spacing, and breakpoints for phone, tablet, laptop, and paper. It builds on #40: the plan document in section order A, the split edit screen, and Shopping and Building as full-screen modes.

Three directions follow, each drawn on the laptop plan and a phone build step. Before them is the measurement everything else rests on: how big text has to be to read at the distances Kerf is used from. After them are the three hard cases the issue names (the cut list on a phone, the structural working, diagrams) and the printed page.

Wide frames scroll sideways inside their box on a phone. Blue stripes and dashed outlines are the grid, drawn over the sketch.

What I need from you

Four choices. My suggestion is first in each. A reply like B with C's title block, distances as proposed, stacked cut list, Letter is enough.

  1. Grid: B content-out layout with C's title block on printed pages, A modular columns with device breakpoints, or C sheets on screen and paper.
  2. Viewing distances: confirm the four distances in the table, or measure your own in the garage. They set the size of everything in the two modes.
  3. Cut list on a phone: stacked rows, a table that scrolls sideways, or priority columns.
  4. Paper: US Letter portrait with landscape diagram pages, or something else your printer takes.

The measurement underneath

How big text must be at each distance

Legibility depends on the angle a character takes up at the eye, not on its size in pixels. Display guidance for control rooms puts the minimum easily readable character height at 16 minutes of arc and the optimal at 20 (Activu, citing MIL-DTL-87268D; human-factors texts give 20 to 22). Height is distance × tan(angle). The table turns that into CSS pixels for a phone and a tablet.

Proposed: two distances in every modeSuggested

Read distance, 0.5 m. Instructions, notes, and method text are read with the phone in your hand: about 20 px on a phone, which is a normal reading size.

Glance distance, 1.0 m. The numbers you cut to (length, saw setting) are read from across the bench with your hands on the board: about 50 px for a length on a phone, at 20′.

A length like 138-3/8" at 50 px is about 230 px wide in tabular figures, which fits a 375 px phone with room to spare. At 1.5 m it would not, so the sawhorse distance is for the tablet only.

Two densities, chosen by context

Desk density for the plan and the edit screen: conventional sizes, read at 0.5 to 0.6 m on a laptop.

Shop density inside Shopping and Building: the read and glance sizes above, and controls at least 56 px tall for gloved hands, above WCAG's 44 px enhanced target.

Density follows the mode, not the screen width, so a phone on the couch reading the plan gets desk density.

Grid

Three spatial systems

All three use a 4 px base unit and keep running text to 45 to 90 characters a line (Butterick's range). They differ in what the layout is measured from: the device, the content, or the sheet of paper.

Predictable and well documented
Fixed columns make alignment easy to check by eye and easy to hand to M5, M6, and M7: "the cut card spans 4 columns". Android's window size classes and most published grids work this way.
Built around devices, not content
The same component lands in different places at each breakpoint. A cut card that appears inside a step, a tablet pane, and the plan needs a rule for each. Between breakpoints the layout does not change at all, so a tablet in split view can fall into an awkward width.
Columns and numbers
Four columns on a phone are too narrow for a label beside a large number; most shop content spans all four, so the grid does little work there.

B · Content-out

Layout primitives that respond to the space they are given, with a fluid spacing scale

No page-wide columns. Every screen is built from a handful of primitives, each deciding its own arrangement from its container's width (Every Layout):

  • Stack: vertical flow with one spacing value. Steps, cards, sections.
  • Sidebar: a narrow and a wide part side by side, stacking when the wide part would drop below a minimum. Contents beside the plan; step beside its diagram on a tablet.
  • Switcher: a row that becomes a column below a set width. The summary's key results; the two launch cards.
  • Cluster: items that wrap like words. Tags, flags, button groups.
  • Center: caps running text at 65 characters.

Spacing is fluid between a 360 px and a 1280 px viewport, each step a clamp() (Utopia):

Components work wherever they are placed
The cut card behaves the same inside a step, in a tablet pane, or in the plan, because it responds to its own width. That matters here: #40 puts the same pieces (cut card, board bar, flag) in several places.
No in-between widths to fall into
Split-screen tablets and narrow laptop windows get a sensible layout without a breakpoint for each. Container queries are widely supported, so primitives can respond to their container rather than the viewport.
Harder to see the grid
There are no columns to point at, so alignment comes from consistent spacing and shared edges rather than a visible structure. Review needs to look at a range of widths, not four.
Fluid sizes and exact numbers
Spacing changes smoothly with width; type for numbers should not, so the shop sizes from the distance table stay fixed and only spacing flows.

C · Sheets

Every view is composed as drawing sheets, the same on screen and on paper

The plan is a set of sheets, the size of US Letter (ANSI A in ASME Y14.1, 8-1/2" x 11"). Each has a border and a title block across the bottom: project, sheet title, sheet number, template version, and date. Inside the border, a 6-column grid: six 100 px columns with 24 px gutters fill the 720 px between 1/2" margins.

On a laptop the sheets sit one below another at their real proportion. On a phone a sheet becomes one column and its title block becomes the header; the sheet number stays, so "sheet 4" means the same thing on paper and on screen.

Sheets in the worked example:

  1. Summary and isometric view
  2. Exploded view
  3. Site layout (landscape)
  4. Structural review (as many sheets as the working needs)
  5. Cut list
  6. Cut details, a part or two per sheet
  7. Boards and shopping list
  8. Build steps
  9. Parameters and inputs

The modes are not sheets; they use the same 6 columns at shop density.

Native to the subject
Plans are drawn on sheets with title blocks. The title block carries what a printed plan needs and the screen hides: which project, which template version, which sheet, printed when. Sheet numbers give you and an experienced builder a shared way to point ("sheet 6, the crossbar").
Strongest print parity
Paper and screen paginate the same way. Named pages let the site layout print in landscape on its own sheet, and every major browser supports them.
Sheets fight the phone
A page-sized chunk is the wrong unit for a scrolling screen. Long content (the structural working runs to several sheets) has to split at sheet boundaries that mean nothing on a phone.
Browsers paginate poorly
CSS can avoid breaks inside a block, but cannot measure what fits on a sheet before printing. Fixed sheets on screen need content split by hand or by script. Running content in the page margins works only in Chrome (131 and later), not in Safari or Firefox, so a title block on every printed page has to be part of the content.

Suggestion

B for every screen, because Kerf reuses the same pieces in different places and on widths no breakpoint list anticipates. Take C's title block for printed pages only: each printed section starts with a block naming the project, section, template version, and print date, and diagrams print on their own landscape pages. A is the conventional choice, and gives nothing here that B lacks except visible columns.

Each check opens to its margin and the two values compared. The working sits under it, each part collapsible, so a phone shows the four margins on one screen and a laptop can open everything.
Formulas never wrap
A wrapped formula is easy to misread. Each formula sits in its own sideways-scrolling block; the page itself never scrolls sideways.
Measure
Assumptions and notes are capped at 65 characters a line on a laptop; tables of inputs use the full width.
Print
Everything is expanded when printed, and a check never breaks across pages if it fits on one.

Hard case 3, and paper

Diagrams and the printed page

Diagrams on screen

A diagram takes the full width of its container, never less than the content column, with its aspect ratio kept. On a phone the site layout rotates to fit portrait if that makes it larger; dimension text keeps a minimum size and the diagram scrolls inside its box rather than shrinking text below it. Pinch-zoom is left to the browser.

Paper: US LetterSuggested

Portrait Letter with 1/2" margins for text sections; landscape Letter for the isometric, exploded, and site layout views, using named pages. Each section starts on a new page with a title block: project, section, template version, print date. Checks, cut details, and steps never split across pages when they fit on one.

Named pages are supported in every major browser since 2023. Running headers in the page margins are Chrome-only, so the title block is part of each section, not a running header.

Paper: the alternatives

Tabloid (11" x 17", ANSI B) gives diagrams twice the area, if your printer takes it. A4 if you print elsewhere. The layout works on any of them; only the page size and diagram scale change.

Character height and viewing distance

Minimum easily readable 16 arc-minutes, optimal 20 (Activu white paper, citing MIL-DTL-87268D). Human-factors references give 20 to 22 for assured legibility. The basis for the distance table.

Layout primitives (Stack, Sidebar, Switcher, Cluster, Center) that respond to available space instead of device breakpoints. Basis for direction B.

Utopia

Fluid type and space scales as clamp() values between a minimum and maximum viewport, with space pairs for larger jumps. Basis for B's spacing scale.
