Kerf brand

Issue #166. Kerf has no logo: the header shows the name in bold text and the browser tab shows a blank page icon. This page explores directions in rounds so one can be chosen before any is refined. Round 2, first below, follows the owner's response to round 1, which is kept further down.

Every sample is drawn the way a final file would be. Marks are plain shapes on a 32-unit square. Wordmarks are the app's own face, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next at weight 700 (decision 0015), converted to outlines, since a favicon or a social preview image cannot load a web font. Colors are the app's tokens: graphite text, chalk-line blue accent, warm paper (Stage 4). Each direction is shown in light and dark, in the app's header at real size, in a browser tab, at 64, 32, and 16 px, and as the 180 px icon a phone uses when the site is added to the home screen.

The enlarged squares under each direction are the favicon drawn at its real pixel size and then magnified without smoothing: 16 px is what a standard screen shows in a tab, 32 px what a Retina screen shows. That is the hardest size any mark has to survive. These are sketches: only round 1's direction 3 is aligned to the 16 px grid so far, and the chosen direction gets that alignment, and a hand-tuned 16 px version if it needs one, when it is refined.

Round 2

The owner's response to round 1 (October 2026). Not the two saws or the dimensioned kerf. The slanted cut (3) was liked, but a square with a slanted stripe reads as the "diver down" flag, the red flag with a white diagonal that marks a diver below. That leaves the cut k (5), liked but worth exploring further, and room for new ideas.

So this round is two things: four more versions of the cut k, compared side by side, then the three strongest as full directions; and two new ideas from the name and the trade.

6. The k with an end cut

The ascender's top is cut at 45° from square instead of flat, the way a board's end is cut at a miter-saw setting. (A 20.0° cut, the swing set's, was tried first and was too slight to see at header size.) There is no slot, so nothing in the k is too thin to survive small sizes; the angled top is the one detail, and it shows down to 16 px.

9. Kerf bending

A new idea from the name. Kerf bending is a real shop technique: a row of kerfs cut across one face lets a straight board bend into a curve. The mark is that board, bent into an arch, with the kerfs on its inside face. At tab size it keeps three kerfs instead of seven.

10. The crow's foot

A new idea from how measurements are marked. Carpenters mark an exact length on a board with a crow's foot, a V whose point lands on the measurement, because a single pencil tick is ambiguous. The mark is that V on a board's edge: precision, which is what the app is for.

Recommendation

Combine 6 and 5: the 45° end cut on the ascender and the kerf between the stem and the arm (the third row of the comparison). Each part does a different job. The end cut is what survives at 16 px, so the tab icon is a k with an angled top that is still distinct from a plain letter. The kerf shows at header size and larger, and makes the cut reading certain rather than a type style. Together they need no symbol beside the name, which fits the advice in the sources below.

There is also a reason the kerf sits where it does: the k's arm and leg form a V whose point lands on the stem, which is exactly a carpenter's crow's foot (direction 10) marking a point on a board. The cut separates the mark from the board. That can stay an unstated detail.

7, the three boards, is the alternative if the owner wants the woodworking reading to be stronger; it costs a busier letter. 8, 9, and 10 each read as a common icon or convention (strikethrough, a gauge, download) and are not recommended.

Recommendation

Direction 3, the cut, as the mark, with the plain "kerf" wordmark beside it. It is the only direction that carries both the name (the kerf is the gap) and what the app is for (exact angled end cuts) in one shape that survives 16 px, in one color, without looking like another icon. Its weakness, that it is abstract, matters less for a tool with one user who will learn it in a day.

Direction 5 is the second choice if a quieter, wordmark-led identity is preferred; its k could take direction 3's slanted cut instead of the vertical one, which would join the two. The saw directions (1 and 2) are the most literal and the least distinctive, and the circular blade reads as a settings gear at tab size. Direction 4 needs two versions of the mark.

Precedents and sources

  • What makes a mark work. Sagi Haviv (Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv) judges a logo by three things: appropriate, distinctive, and simple, and says a symbol needs a reason (a long name, divisions to unite, a need for shorthand); without one, the name itself should carry the identity. Logo Design Love, interview with Sagi Haviv. Kerf's reason for a symbol is the favicon and the home-screen icon, which a four-letter word cannot fill.
  • A gap that carries meaning. The FedEx wordmark (Lindon Leader at Landor, 1994) hides an arrow in the space between the E and the x; Leader has said it was found during critique, not set out as a goal. Directions 3 to 5 use the same device: the kerf is negative space. CNN, "Hidden designs in famous logos".
  • Which files a site needs. A 32 px favicon.ico, an SVG icon that can switch colors with prefers-color-scheme, and a 180 px apple-touch-icon.png; the 192 and 512 px icons and the manifest are for installable web apps only. Evil Martians, "How to Favicon in 2026". Installable web apps are a non-goal in PRODUCT.md, so the manifest icons are left out.
  • Designing for 16 px. Reduce the mark to one shape and one color, use negative space to define it, and check it at actual size, not zoomed. SitePoint, "Perfecting your website's favicon"; 10Web, favicon design (a vendor's blog, so taken as practice, not authority).
  • No brands. Hard rule 11 keeps tool suggestions generic. The saw directions are drawn as generic shapes (every handsaw and every circular blade has these outlines), not any maker's tool.
