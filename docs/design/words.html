Common to all three

What no voice changes

Numbers come from core

Text never contains a number core did not produce, and never rounds or rewrites one (hard rules 1 and 4). Words around a number, not the number, carry the voice.

Margins, not verdicts

"Margin 1.1x under these assumptions", never safe, unsafe, pass, fail, or an adjective that judges (hard rule 2).

Nothing filled in

Text never suggests a value for a structural input or calls one a default (hard rule 3).

Generic tools

Tool types, never brands or products (hard rule 11).

Angles with their reference

"Saw at 20.0°" or "20.0° to the edge", never a bare "20° cut" (DOMAIN.md, Units).

Sources stay

Safety-relevant text keeps its source and page, such as "(CPSC Pub. 324, p. 3)" (hard rule 5).

View

V1 · Shop voice · Suggested

Plain and direct: the verb first, the number, then why

Tone (NN/g's four dimensions): casual-leaning neutral, serious, respectful, matter-of-fact.

From the subject
How good shop instructions and safety handbooks talk to someone with a tool in hand: a verb first, one action, the number. The CPSC home playground handbook writes to parents this way: "Check nuts and bolts twice a month and tighten as needed."
Principles
Plain language: active voice "makes it clear who should do what" (digital.gov), and "you" for the reader. GOV.UK: error messages say what happened and how to fix it. NN/g: errors are precise and constructive, with no blame. Each kind of text has one sentence pattern, so a reader learns where the number and the why will be.
In use
Garage: the verb and the number come first, so a step reads at arm's length in one look. Laptop: the explanation of a margin is one sentence, once. Print: reads the same. The flag tells the reader what to ask and why, without saying the answer.
The risk is terseness that drops the reason. The guide below requires a "why" on every flag and checklist item, and on any step whose reason is not obvious.
Cost
Close to what Kerf already says: the audit below proposes 15 changes to the M4 screens, most of them small. The structural working (assumptions, formulas, steps) keeps the exact, impersonal register it has now, because it is a record of a calculation, not an instruction.

V2 · Report voice

Impersonal and exact, like an engineering memo

Tone: formal, serious, respectful, matter-of-fact.

From the subject
Engineering reports and drawing notes: labeled fields, nouns, the passive voice, nothing addressed to the reader. Kerf's structural working already reads this way ("Supported at the two frame centerlines...").
Principles
Exact and compact on a laptop, and it never sounds like advice. But plain-language guidance warns that the passive voice "obscures who handles what", and runs of nouns ("ground-contact rating of the leg lumber") take longer to read than the verb they hide.
In use
Garage: the reader has to turn labels into an action ("End cuts: top and foot, level...") with a saw in hand. Errors read as blame ("Invalid input"), which NN/g advises against, and a raw range in decimal inches does not match the plan. "Expert review" and "required" lean toward a verdict on the design.
Cost
None for the working, which keeps it in any case. Everything a person acts on would need a translation step in their head.

V3 · Mentor voice

Warm and encouraging, like a friend in the shop

Tone: casual, light, respectful, enthusiastic.

From the subject
Friendly consumer apps and maker blogs: contractions, exclamation marks, encouragement, a sense of someone beside you.
Principles
NN/g found a casual, enthusiastic tone changes how friendly a product feels. Their error guidelines also warn that humor wears thin when the same message appears again and again.
In use
Reassurance slides into verdicts, which hard rule 2 forbids: "perfectly level", "a little headroom", "your friend here" all judge the design. Encouragement is longer to read at arm's length and adds nothing a second time. The flag no longer names the person to ask in the same words as the checklist. One owner, building one swing set, does not need cheering on; they need the number.
Cost
Every string would be rewritten, and each needs checking against hard rule 2 by hand.

One word

"Sanity checks"

PRODUCT.md, AGENTS.md, DOMAIN.md, decision 0001, and the structural review call Kerf's checks "sanity checks". The phrase says the checks are rough, which is the right warning, but it is vague about what is checked, and Google's developer style guide drops it ("Don't use", suggesting "quick check" or "confidence check"). The structural review section already says "not an engineered design", which carries the warning in plain words.

Structural checksSuggested

Says what is checked, matches the "Structural review" section and "structural inputs", and leaves the warning to the sentence that already gives it. Changed in the app (in #45), and in PRODUCT.md, AGENTS.md, DOMAIN.md, ARCHITECTURE.md, and MILESTONES.md when this stage is recorded. Decision 0001 keeps its wording, since decision records are history. Code comments follow as they are touched.

Keep "sanity checks"

Familiar to engineers, and the word itself warns that the checks are rough. No change to the docs.

If V1 is chosen

The content guide

One pattern for each kind of text. On a decision this moves to docs/design/content.md, where M6's method and flag text is written against it.

Voice

Write to the reader as "you", in the active voice, with the verb first in instructions. Kerf is "Kerf" when it says what it did ("Kerf could not reach the server"), never "we" or "I". Write "cannot", not "can't". Sentence case everywhere, including buttons and headings.

Two registers

Everything a person acts on (steps, methods, flags, checklist items, errors, buttons) is in the shop voice. The structural working (assumptions, formulas, intermediate steps, factor reasons) keeps the exact, impersonal register it has now, because it is a record of a calculation.

Method steps

One action per step: the verb, then the number, then the reference ("from the top cut", "on the outside face"). A reason, if the step needs one, follows in its own sentence.

Set the miter saw to 20.0°.

Common mistakes

The mistake as it happens, then how to avoid it, in two sentences.

Marking the overall length, 131-1/2", along an edge leaves the leg too long. Mark 130-1/4" along each edge from the top cut.

Expert checks

The label "Expert check", then what to ask an experienced builder, as "Ask whether..." or "Confirm...", naming the numbers involved. Then "Why:" and one sentence on what the element does. Never the answer, never "make sure it is fine".

Checklist items

The element, then what to confirm, then why: the same three parts core already produces.

Margins

"margin 1.1x under these assumptions", with no adjective, no color (#43, rule 1), and no comparison word such as "only" or "plenty". The page says once what a margin is.

Input needed

"Input needed", then the input's name, then what it decides.

Input needed: deflection limit. It decides the deflection check.

Errors

Say what to enter, not what went wrong; name the field; give the limit in the plan's own format (#67). Empty required field: "Enter..." Out of range: "Beam height must be from 5' 0" to 12' 0"." Not readable: the format, with an example. Never "invalid", "error", or "oops", and no blame (NN/g).

Connection and server problems

What happened, from Kerf's side, and what to do next. Say what is kept.

Not saved. Kerf could not reach the server. Your changes are still here.

Empty screens

What is missing, then how to start.

No projects yet. Start one with a New button above.

Buttons and links

A verb and the thing it acts on, the same words for the same action everywhere: "Save changes", "Delete project", "Start building". A destructive button names what it destroys.

Status

Short, present or past tense, saying what happened: "Saving…", "Saved.", "Loading project…". The ellipsis is one character.

Headings

Nouns for what the section holds: "Cut list", "Structural review", "Dimensions and hardware".

Word list

Use

DOMAIN.md's terms, the same way every time: part, stock, board, offcut, end cut, saw at (setting), angle to the edge, long point, grip, method, tool type, build step, plan, template, regenerate, margin, Expert check, Input needed, structural check (if chosen).

Never: safe, unsafe, pass, fail

Or "OK", "good", "fine", "strong enough", "adequate", "plenty", "only" about a structural result. Hard rule 2: margins, not verdicts.

Never: approved, certified, code-compliant, engineered, guaranteed

Kerf is not an engineer and does not check codes. "Not an engineered design" is the one use of "engineered".

Never: warning, danger, caution as labels

They are the signal words of ANSI Z535 hazard signs (#43). A flag is a question for an experienced builder, labeled "Expert check".

Never: default, suggested value

For a structural input (hard rule 3). Nothing is filled in.

Never: invalid, error, oops

In messages to the reader. Say what to enter (NN/g, GOV.UK).

Never: simply, just, easy, obviously, please

Filler, and what is easy for one person is not for another (Google developer style guide). Instructions are instructions; "please" adds nothing.

Never: sanity check

If structural checks is chosen (Google developer style guide).

Never: brand or product names

Hard rule 11.

"About" only where core says so

Approximation words appear only where core marks a value as approximate, such as the offcut after saw cuts.

Precedents and guidance

Sources

Checked in October 2026.

Plain language

Active voice makes clear who does what; address the reader directly; short words and short sections.

digital.gov: Writing for understanding (formerly plainlanguage.gov)

Error messages

GOV.UK: say what happened and how to fix it, with a different message for each problem. NN/g: precise, constructive, no blame, no humor, no jargon or codes.

GOV.UK: Error message · NN/g: Error-message guidelines

Tone

NN/g's four dimensions: formal or casual, serious or funny, respectful or irreverent, matter-of-fact or enthusiastic. Changing only the tone changed how friendly and formal people rated a site.

NN/g: The four dimensions of tone of voice

Word list

Google's developer documentation style guide: avoid "just", "easy", "please" in instructions, and do not use "sanity check".

Google developer documentation style guide: word list

Writing to a builder

The CPSC home playground handbook gives parents short imperative checks, verb first, with how often.

CPSC Outdoor Home Playground Safety Handbook, p. 5

Hazard signal words

DANGER, WARNING, and CAUTION are the ANSI Z535 signal words for hazards; #43 kept them out of flags.
